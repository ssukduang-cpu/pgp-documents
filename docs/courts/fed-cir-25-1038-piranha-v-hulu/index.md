                        II. DISCUSSION
      We apply the law of the regional circuit to review a dis-
 trict court’s grant of a motion to dismiss. See Bot M8 LLC
 v. Sony Corp. of Am., 4 F.4th 1342, 1353 (Fed. Cir. 2021).
 The Ninth Circuit reviews the grant of a Rule 12(b)(6) mo-
 tion to dismiss de novo, accepting all factual allegations in
 the complaint as true and construing the pleadings in the
 light most favorable to the nonmovant. Id. (citing Knievel
 v. ESPN, 393 F.3d 1068, 1072 (9th Cir. 2005)).
     To determine whether a patent claim is directed to pa-
 tent-ineligible subject matter under 35 U.S.C. § 101, we ap-
 ply the two-step framework set forth by the Supreme Court
 in Mayo Collaborative Services v. Prometheus Laboratories,
 Inc., 566 U.S. 66 (2012) and Alice Corp. Pty. Ltd. v. CLS
 Bank International, 573 U.S. 208 (2014). At step one, we
 determine whether the claims at issue are “directed to” a

 PIRANHA MEDIA DISTRIBUTION LLC v. HULU, LLC                 7

 patent-ineligible concept. Alice, 573 U.S. at 217. At step
 two, we “consider the elements of each claim both individ-
 ually and ‘as an ordered combination’ to determine
 whether the additional elements ‘transform the nature of
 the claim’ into a patent-eligible application.” Id. (quoting
 Mayo, 566 U.S. at 78–79). The Supreme Court has de-
 scribed the step two analysis “as a search for an ‘inventive
 concept.’” Id. at 217 (quoting Mayo, 566 U.S. at 72).
      Piranha makes two principal arguments on ap-
 peal: (1) the district court erred in determining that the
 asserted claims are directed to an abstract idea under Al-
 ice/Mayo step one, Appellant’s Br. 23–45; and (2) the dis-
 trict court erred in holding that the asserted claims did not
 contain an inventive concept under Alice/Mayo step two,
 Appellant’s Br. 46–54. Piranha’s arguments regarding the
 asserted claims are focused on representative claim 1 of the
 ’403 patent. Appellant’s Br. 23. We address each argu-
 ment in turn.
                              A.
     Piranha first argues that the district court erred in con-
 cluding that representative claim 1 of the ’403 patent is di-
 rected to an abstract idea at Alice/Mayo step one.
 Appellant’s Br. 23–45. We disagree and conclude that
 claim 1 of the ’403 patent is directed to the abstract idea of
 preventing skipping advertisements by receiving, organiz-
 ing, displaying, and controlling access to data.
     At Alice/Mayo step one, we “determine whether the
 claims at issue are directed to one of those patent-ineligible
 concepts,” such as an abstract idea. Alice, 573 U.S. at 217.
 To determine whether a claim is “directed to” a patent-in-
 eligible abstract idea, “we look to whether the claim[ ] ‘fo-
 cus[es] on a specific means or method that improves the
 relevant technology or [is] instead directed to a result or
 effect that itself is the abstract idea and merely invoke[s]
 generic processes and machinery.’” CardioNet, LLC v. In-

Case: 25-1038     Document: 46      Page: 8     Filed: 10/14/2026

 8               PIRANHA MEDIA DISTRIBUTION LLC v. HULU, LLC

 foBionic, Inc., 955 F.3d 1358, 1368 (Fed. Cir. 2020) (quot-
 ing McRo, Inc. v. Bandai Namco Games Am., Inc., 837 F.3d
 1299, 1314 (Fed. Cir. 2016)). For software-based inven-
 tions, Alice/Mayo step one “often turns on whether the
 claim[ ] focus[es] on the specific asserted improvement in
 computer capabilities or, instead, on a process that quali-
 fies as an abstract idea for which computers are invoked
 merely as a tool.” In re Killian, 45 F.4th 1373, 1382
 (Fed. Cir. 2022) (cleaned up).
      The plain language of claim 1 of the ’403 patent shows
 that the claims are directed to an abstract idea. “As we
 have by now frequently held, claims reciting generalized
 steps of collecting, analyzing, and presenting information,
 using nothing other than the conventional operations of ge-
 neric computer components, are directed to abstract ideas.”
 Mobile Acuity Ltd. v. Blippar Ltd., 110 F.4th 1280, 1293
 (Fed. Cir. 2024). Claim 1 recites “one or more processors
 for receiving data,” here “digital media content and digital
 advertising content.” ’403 patent col. 44 ll. 5–6. Claim 1
 provides that a piece of software called the “intersplicer”
 sequences the digital media content and digital advertising
 content into a “stream” and a “user interface output”
 causes presentation of the data to the user. Id. col. 44
 ll. 10–18, 52–58. The claim also recites a memory for stor-
 ing a setting, where if the setting “indicate[s] presentation
 of a required part of the digital advertising content,” “at
 least one of a user volume control or a user navigation con-
 trol” is disabled. Id. col. 44 ll. 59 to col. 45 l. 2. The claim
 achieves the goal of preventing skipping advertisements
 via steps that do nothing more than receive, organize, dis-
 play, and control access to data, which places the claim
 squarely in line with those claims we have previously de-
 termined to be directed to computer-implemented abstract
 ideas. See Trinity Info Media, LLC v. Covalent, Inc.,
 72 F.4th 1355, 1361–62, 1365 (Fed. Cir. 2023) (concluding
 that claim was “directed to the abstract idea of matching
 based on questioning” when the recited steps performed

 the actions of “collecting information, analyzing it, and dis-
 playing certain results”); Miller Mendel, Inc. v. City of
 Anna, 107 F.4th 1345, 1352 (Fed. Cir. 2024) (concluding
 that asserted claims are “directed to the abstract idea of
 performing a background check” where asserted claims re-
 cited steps of “receiving, storing, transmitting, determin-
 ing, selecting, and generating information”).
      On appeal, Piranha argues that claim 1 of the ’403 pa-
 tent is not directed to an abstract idea because the claim
 provides a specific technological solution to the technologi-
 cal problem of “users’ easily manipulating media to avoid
 ads.” Appellant’s Br. 24, 23–45. The ’403 patent and Pira-
 nha’s own statements to the district court belie Piranha’s
 framing of claim 1. The ’403 patent recognizes that “tape
 recorders, video cassette recorders, compact disk burners,
 digital video disk burners, and computer systems” all allow
 users to “manipulat[e]” media to bypass media owners’
 monetization strategies. ’403 patent col. 3 ll. 17–19, col. 2
 l. 62 to col. 3 l. 6. Consumers have used analog technology
 to skip advertisements in media for decades. Piranha’s
 own filings at the district court confirm that “copyright
 holders . . . giving away their content for free . . . was a
 problem associated with many, if not all, forms of pre-ex-
 isting media distribution. For example, broadcast, cable,
 internet, and satellite broadcasts could be recorded and
 during playback, ads could be fast forwarded.” J.A. 552
 (emphasis added). We conclude that claim 1 does not solve
 a technological problem.
      The Asserted Patents’ shared specification confirms
 that claim 1 is directed to an abstract idea. The specifica-
 tion describes the “object of the . . . invention” as
 “provid[ing] a revenue generating market for the free con-
 sumer redistribution of media content, sponsored through
 paid advertising that a consumer may be required to expe-
 rience during the course of media playback.” ’403 patent
 col. 6 ll. 26–30. The invention is accomplished by a system

 “that offers the benefits of content restriction and con-
 trolled playback.” Id. col. 6 ll. 30–32. “Various embodi-
 ments of the invention facilitate the wide distribution of
 media content in a way that allows the free use of media
 properties, on demand . . . while at the same time enabling
 compensation to copyright holders.” Id. Abstract. These
 parts of the specification support that the district court cor-
 rectly determined that claim 1 is not directed to solving a
 technological problem. Decision at 934–36. The specifica-
 tion confirms that claim 1 is directed to an abstract idea at
 step one.
     Piranha’s cited cases are readily distinguishable. Re-
 gardless of the ease with which users can skip advertise-
 ments, their ability to do so is unlike the problems we found
 to be technological in any of the cases Piranha cites. See
 Appellant’s Br. 32–35. For example, in Data Engine Tech-
 nologies LLC v. Google LLC, the claims addressed the prob-
 lem that “[u]sers were required to master complex
 commands” to navigate “three-dimensional or multipage
 electronic spreadsheets.” 906 F.3d 999, 1002 (Fed. Cir.
 2018). The operation of “computers and prior art electronic
 spreadsheets” created the specific “technological” problem.
 Id. at 1008. Other cases Piranha cites involve claims that
 “purport[ ] to meet a challenge unique to computer net-
 works.” Packet Intel. LLC v. NetScout Sys., Inc., 965 F.3d
 1299, 1309 (Fed. Cir. 2020); see also SRI Int’l, Inc. v. Cisco
 Sys., Inc., 930 F.3d 1295, 1303 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (identifying
 “a technological problem arising in computer networks:
 identifying hackers or potential intruders into the net-
 work”); Uniloc USA, Inc. v. LG Elecs. USA, Inc., 957 F.3d
 1303, 1305, 1307–08 (Fed. Cir. 2020) (holding the “claims
 at issue are directed to a patent-eligible improvement to
 computer functionality” where they “overcome a problem,”
 latency, “specifically arising in the realm of computer net-
 works”). Piranha also cites Finjan v. Blue Coat Systems
 and Ancora Technologies v. HTC, both of which involved
 claims related to the security of computer files. Appellant’s

 Br. 33–34; Ancora Techs., Inc. v. HTC Am., Inc., 908 F.3d
 1343, 1344, 1348–49 (Fed. Cir. 2018) (discussing claim ad-
 dressing a technological problem of “vulnerability of li-
 cense-authorization software to hacking”); Finjan, Inc.
 v. Blue Coat Sys., Inc., 879 F.3d 1299, 1304–05 (Fed. Cir.
 2018) (discussing claim addressing problem of scanning for
 malicious computer viruses). Advertisement skipping with
 relative ease is not a problem uniquely related to the oper-
 ation of computer or network technology.
       Claim 1 is also not directed to a “specific asserted im-
 provement in computer capabilities.” Trinity Info Media,
 72 F.4th at 1363–64. Piranha argues that the district court
 erroneously concluded that claim 1 is not directed to a spe-
 cific improvement in computer capabilities because the dis-
 trict court overgeneralized the actions of the claimed
 intersplicer. Appellant’s Br. 36, 35–42. We disagree. Pi-
 ranha’s own description of the intersplicer’s purportedly
 patent-eligible activity is nothing more than the organiza-
 tion of data. See Appellant’s Br. 36 (“adaptively select, se-
 quence, and resequence ads that are shown on the basis of
 user navigation”); id. at 37 (“step-by-step instructions for
 sequencing and adaptively resequencing media and ad con-
 tent in response to user inputs”); id. at 39 (“applies rules
 meeting criteria specified in the claims to sequence and
 adaptively resequence data blocks of media content and ad
 content on the fly depending how a user manipulates the
 playback position”). To the extent Piranha argues that
 claim 1 is distinguishable from other claims because the
 human mind cannot perform the steps taken by the inter-
 splicer, Appellant’s Br. 39, we have specifically held that
 “the inability for the human mind to perform each claim
 step does not alone confer patentability.” FairWarning IP,
 LLC v. Iatric Sys., Inc., 839 F.3d 1089, 1098 (Fed. Cir.
 2016). Accordingly, we disagree with Piranha that the dis-
 trict court overgeneralized the steps of claim 1 and con-
 clude that claim 1 does not provide a technological solution
 to a technological problem.

      Finally, Piranha argues that we should conclude that
 claim 1 is patent eligible at step one because it purportedly
 does not claim solely the result of preventing users from
 skipping advertisements or broadly preempt the field of
 preventing users from skipping advertisements. Appel-
 lant’s Br. 40–45. But “the absence of complete preemption
 does not demonstrate patent eligibility.” Ariosa Diagnos-
 tics, Inc. v. Sequenom, Inc., 788 F.3d 1371, 1379 (Fed. Cir.
 2015); see also OIP Techs., Inc. v. Amazon.com, Inc.,
 788 F.3d 1359, 1362–63 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (“[T]hat the claims
 do not preempt all price optimization or may be limited to
 price optimization in the e-commerce setting do not make
 them any less abstract.”). In sum, at Alice/Mayo step one,
 claim 1 is directed to an abstract idea.
                              B.
      We next turn to Alice/Mayo step two, in which we “con-
 sider the elements of [the] claim both individually and ‘as
 an ordered combination’ to determine whether the addi-
 tional elements ‘transform the nature of the claim’ into a
 patent-eligible application.” Alice, 573 U.S. at 217 (quoting
 Mayo, 566 U.S. at 79, 78). Here, the limitations of claim 1,
 individually or as an ordered combination, recite “‘well-un-
 derstood, routine conventional activit[ies],’ either by re-
 quiring conventional computer activities or routine data-
 gathering steps.” OIP Techs., 788 F.3d at 1363 (quoting Al-
 ice, 573 U.S. at 225).
     The language of claim 1 and the specification confirm
 that the invention uses only generic, conventional com-
 puter components “operating according to their ordinary
 functions.”     Two-Way Media Ltd. v. Comcast Cable
 Commc’ns, LLC, 874 F.3d 1329, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2017).
 Claim 1 recites “[a] digital media player device” that com-
 prises “one or more processors,” a “user interface input,” a
 “user interface output,” and at least one memory. ’403 pa-
 tent col. 44 ll. 2, 5–9, 52, 59. The specification confirms
 that “the invention applies to . . . hardware components

Case: 25-1038    Document: 46      Page: 13    Filed: 10/14/2026

 PIRANHA MEDIA DISTRIBUTION LLC v. HULU, LLC                13

 that may be employed in a consumer-grade computer sys-
 tem or other digital device to carry out the ends of the me-
 dia player/viewer software.” Id. col. 13 ll. 8–13. The
 claim’s use of conventional computing components used in
 an expected manner do not provide an inventive concept.
     We are not persuaded by Piranha’s argument that
 claim 1 includes an inventive concept because the claimed
 intersplicer “offer[s] a specific improvement over the prior
 art.” Appellant’s Br. 46, 46–52. The intersplicer performs
 only insignificant data gathering steps. See, e.g., ’403 pa-
 tent col. 44 ll. 10–51. The intersplicer “sequenc[es]” digital
 media and digital advertising content, id. col. 44 ll. 14–18,
 and “adaptively modif[ies]” the position of the digital ad-
 vertising content to a new position when a user updates the
 play position in the content sequence, id. col. 44 ll. 23–34,
 based on “adaptive preference rules,” id. col. 44 ll. 35–42.
 The content of these “adaptive preference rules” is that the
 new advertising position be “a predetermined length of
 time” or “predetermined number of digital media blocks”
 after the user’s new play position. Id. col. 44 ll. 43–51.
 These steps are like “an unspecified set of rules for display-
 ing and organizing [data] . . . we have elsewhere explained
 impart no inventive concept.” Intell. Ventures I LLC
 v. Cap. One Fin. Corp., 850 F.3d 1332, 1342 (Fed. Cir.
 2017). The intersplicer’s insignificant data gathering steps
 are insufficient to confer patent eligibility at Alice/Mayo
 step two.
     Piranha also essentially repeats its Alice/Mayo step
 one argument that the intersplicer provides a technological
 solution to a technological problem in an attempt to analo-
 gize claim 1 to other claims Piranha contends we have
 found to cover “an unconventional technological solu-
 tion . . . to a technological problem.” Appellant’s Br. 47,
 46–48 (citing Amdocs (Isr.) Ltd. v. Openet Telecom, Inc.,
 841 F.3d 1288, 1300 (Fed. Cir. 2016)). But as described
 above, claim 1 (including the steps taken by the inter-

                               C.
      We do not reach whether the Board decision has any
 preclusive effect. The district court explicitly did not rely
 on preclusion and instead considered the Board decision
 only “to the extent that the [c]ourt [found] the reasoning
 persuasive and relevant to the analysis of the ’403 patent.”
 Decision at 934. Appellate courts generally do not reach
 alternative arguments “that were not the basis for the de-
 cision below.” Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Epstein,
 516 U.S. 367, 379 n.5 (1996). Accordingly, we do not reach
 this issue.
